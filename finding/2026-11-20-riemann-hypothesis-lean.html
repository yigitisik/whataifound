Lean proof of the Riemann hypothesis and GRH for Dirichlet L-functions, released without a paper

Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic, says he found with Claude a proof that every nontrivial zero of the Riemann zeta function, and of every Dirichlet L-function, has real part one half, and has released it only as a Lean formalization of about 17 million lines that he says he does not yet fully understand.

What was found

Riemann conjectured in 1859 that every nontrivial zero of ζ(s)\zeta(s) has real part 1/21/2, and the generalized Riemann hypothesis makes the same assertion for Dirichlet L-functions. The release is a Lean 4 project and a four-page note, with no paper. Its Main.lean proves riemannHypothesis, whose type is Mathlib's own RiemannHypothesis (which excludes the trivial zeros and the point s=1s = 1), together with four companion theorems: no Dirichlet L-function vanishes in ℜs>1/2\Re s > 1/2, for every modulus and every character, imprimitive ones included, with the pole of the principal character at s=1s = 1 excepted; and every zero of one with 0<ℜs<10 < \Re s < 1 lies on ℜs=1/2\Re s = 1/2, stated again separately for primitive characters. The project pins Lean 4.33.1 and Mathlib's v4.33.1 tag and runs to 175,187 files and about 17.4 million lines, with no comments and 818,755 theorem or lemma declarations, every file in the import closure of Main. RH itself is derived from the nonvanishing of all Dirichlet L-functions to the right of 1/21/2 by the standard functional-equation argument, using Mathlib's lemmas. The note gives the strategy only in outline. The argument descends from Stepanov's method for the Riemann hypothesis over function fields, where a divisor with multiplicity does the work; over the integers multiplicity would be something like differentiating whole numbers, and it is imitated instead by a chain of theta lifts between totally definite unitary groups, a chain of mm lifts winning multiplicity mm. For a von Mangoldt sum of length XX the chain is taken about X\sqrt{X} long, a choice the note attributes to Claude. Other ingredients it names include Gan-Gross-Prasad periods, a relative trace formula, an Iwasawa tower, the Bost slope inequality and a weakening of Vandiver's conjecture. A timeline the note says Claude reconstructed puts the first step, a Stepanov-type determinant, on 6 October 2026 and GRH on 16 November 2026.

arXiv, 2026-11-20: ti:"Riemann hypothesis" AND (abs:proof OR ti:proof) returned 95 records, the most recent being a proof of the quasi-Riemann hypothesis using ℚ[i]\mathbb{Q}[i] (2611.16665) and a two-interval criterion for RH (2611.10726); the claimed proofs of RH itself among them (for example 2607.04338 and 2408.13292) have no standing in the literature, and none comes with a formalization. MathDB records the problem as 315854, marked claimed solved and unverified on 20 November. Palomar's 200 most recent records (through PALOMAR-2026-11-21-000005), ProofAtlas's formalizations and vibemathed's dataset held no registration on the same day. Within this registry, the Dirichlet half of 2026-10-06-quasi-riemann-hypothesis would follow from this claim, but the Hecke L-functions over ℚ(−3)\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3}) in that entry and the Dedekind zeta functions in 2026-10-12-dedekind-zeta-quasi-riemann are outside what this Lean states, and the simplicity of zeros in 2026-08-10-zeta-zeros-critical-line, on which Alpöge is an author, does not follow from RH at all. A second, unrelated claim of GRH appeared the same day: Ravi Vakil posted a draft by Alessandro Maria Masullo, which he describes as all human with no AI and which Brian Conrad had been checking for months, and added about eight hours later that several people had reported problems in it found with the help of AI models. That claim is outside this registry's scope and has no formalization.

Caveats and known objections

Graded formal for exactly the five statements in Main.lean, which is the move this entry said a completed independent Comparator run would justify: the headline theorem is Mathlib's RiemannHypothesis verbatim and the challenge file imports only Mathlib. Two such runs have now completed, both by Andrew Sutherland. The unmodified Comparator v4.33.0 binary, with the repository's own comparator.json, passed its statement comparison and axiom check, had nanoda and Lean's default kernel accept, and reported 'Your solution is okay!' on 23 November after 64 hours; that run reused the original build, with its lake build step satisfied by a stub. lake comparator under Lean 4.36.0, run on the canonical challenge export, passed the same stages with Lean's paranoid kernel, nanoda and the default kernel all accepting, and finished on 25 November after 122 hours. Because the 4.36 kernels postdate the 4.34.1 runtime fixes, that run answers the earlier worry that the pinned Lean 4.33.1 could be fooled by deliberately constructed input. A separate sandboxed lake build on another machine, with no network and a read-only source tree, reproduced the 61.6 GB export byte for byte. Michael Rubinstein (Waterloo) independently compiled every module, replayed each through the kernel and ran nanoda, with no errors. On 25 November Luke Sernau and colleagues at Anthropic posted a rewrite of the same five theorems in about 300,000 lines (readable edition) and 76,000 lines (compact edition) under Lean 4.35.0, which lake comparator --paranoid, running every external checker shipped with Lean, accepted on Sutherland's machines and on Rubinstein's; one of Sutherland's two runs on the readable edition was stopped for lack of memory, not rejected. The leanprover-community repository now maintains that readable edition. What the grade does not cover is understanding. There is still no paper and no human is on record as having read the argument: the shorter edition's own README says no mathematician has read it through, and Rubinstein's 265-page working guide to the zeta case, written with Claude, Codex and Gemini, reduces the main inequality to thirteen numbered hypotheses it does not prove and calls itself a work in progress. The author's note says he does not yet master the surrounding mathematics, that parts of his own timeline paraphrase a summary by Claude, and that a written version ran past 250,000 pages. Clay's rules require journal publication and two years of scrutiny, so this settles the statements in Lean and not the prize. On scope, GRH here means Dirichlet L-functions only: Dedekind zeta functions and Hecke or automorphic L-functions are not stated, and the Extended Riemann Hypothesis is something the note only expects. The original source defines tactics, kexactB among them, that assign a term without the elaborator checking its type, which is why the independent kernels rather than the build carry the weight. The original archive was published without a licence; the shorter edition is Apache 2.0, copyright Anthropic. Anthropic itself has made no announcement, and AFP reports that the announcement came the day after Anthropic's stock market debut. Autonomy is graded collaborative on the strictest defensible reading. The note credits Claude with the decisive technical work, including the length of the lift chain and the parts the author says he does not yet understand, which would support ai-led; but it also says he set the strategy of combining two ideas from a toy setting and steered the work to the analogous situation, which is human research input beyond posing the problem, so the weaker grade wins. The note names only Claude; Tao describes the model as internal, and no version is stated.

Independent checks

Andrew Sutherland (MIT), checks run with Claude Code: All checks finished by 25 November 2026. All 175,187 modules compile under Lean 4.33.1 with zero errors; the five theorems depend only on propext, Classical.choice and Quot.sound; a sandboxed lake build on a separate machine reproduced the export byte for byte; leanchecker replays every module; Lean's default and paranoid kernels, nanoda, con-leche and lean4lean accept; the compare modes of con-leche and con-ron confirm all five theorems against the Mathlib-only challenge; Comparator v4.33.0 and lake comparator under Lean 4.36.0 both report 'Your solution is okay!'. Combined with the independent Robin1984 formalization, the proof also yields Robin's inequality for every n above 5040 and Lagarias's criterion as hypothesis-free theorems, checked the same way. · link ↗

Andrew Sutherland (MIT), on the shorter edition of 25 November: Both editions of lsernau/grh-lean at commit 72fead10 compile in a sandbox under Lean 4.35.0 with the three standard axioms only, every module replays through the kernel, and lake comparator --paranoid, which runs nanoda, Lean's paranoid kernel, lean4lean, con-leche, con-ron and the default kernel, reported 'Your solution is okay!' for both, the compact edition on two machines. · link ↗

Michael Rubinstein (University of Waterloo), checks run with Claude Code: On the original archive, 21 November: all 175,187 modules compile, the five theorems use the three standard axioms only, the kernel replay accepts every module and nanoda accepts the 1,484,729 declarations the theorems depend on. On the compact edition, 26 November: lake comparator --paranoid accepted with all six kernels, run without Comparator's sandbox, which the cluster does not allow. · link ↗

Maintainers of leanprover-community/riemann-hypothesis: Mirrored the archive unmodified, wrote a Comparator challenge stating the five theorems against Mathlib alone, read the statements by hand, checked every dependency pin, and ran a static scan that found no axiom, sorry, admit, native_decide, implemented_by, extern, unsafe or opaque, while listing the metaprogramming that bypasses the elaborator. The repository moved to the shorter readable edition on 25 November; these notes are kept on its alpoge branch. · link ↗
